Project summary

The two-armed risky-choice task

Each trial the patient chooses one of two arms:

  • Gamble  big reward with probability P, otherwise nothing.
    P steps in blocks of ~25 trials across {0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8}.
  • Safe  a small certain reward.

Reward magnitudes are not stored in the exports, so modelling assumes the canonical task ratio 4 : 1 (gamble : safe). Choice is read from the reliable ChosenArm column; non-responding trials are excluded.

Aim

Two questions

1 · Selectivity

Are there neurons that selectively respond to the choice (which arm) or to the reward (the outcome)?

2 · Latent-variable coding

Are firing rates related to the perceived probability of reward (the belief before the outcome) or to the prediction error (the surprise after the outcome)?

Question 2 needs latent variables we cannot observe directly — they come from a behavioural model. This is the model → brain bridge that motivates the second half of the talk.

Spike-timing analysis

ZETA — testing for time-locked spiking

The classic PSTH forces an arbitrary choice — the bin size — and the answer can change with it.

ZETA (Zenith of Event-based Time-locked Anomalies) is binning-free and parameter-free:

  1. Pool spike times relative to the event across all trials.
  2. Build their cumulative distribution.
  3. Compare to the flat line expected if spikes were uniformly spread (no time-locking).
  4. The largest deviation from that line is ZETA; its timing is the zenith.
  5. Significance from jittered resampling of event times.

One-sample vs. two-sample ZETA

One-sample → responsiveness

Is the neuron time-locked to an event at all?

spike timing vs. a uniform null around one event.

Two-sample (ZETA2) → selectivity

Does the time-locked response differ between two trial types?

e.g. rewarded vs. not (outcome)  ·  gamble vs. safe (choice).

The two-sample test is the one we trust: it holds timing fixed and subtracts the shared baseline, so slow drift and rate differences cancel out. (Why — see the limitations slide.)

ZETA results

A limitation to respect

The one-sample test reports ~90% of units "responsive" to almost every event:

Aligned to% responsive (FDR)
Trial start89.4%
Cue92.0%
Cue → reward93.4%
Reward onset75.7%

With ~30k spikes/unit, ZETA flags any non-flat firing — slow drift, rhythmicity, motion. The near-identical rates across four different events are the tell-tale sign.

What we do about it

  • Treat one-sample responsiveness only as a coarse "channel is alive" filter.
  • Put the scientific weight on the two-sample contrasts — the shared baseline cancels, so drift can't masquerade as selectivity.
  • The numbers on the previous slide are all two-sample.

Independently flagged and confirmed by adversarial re-derivation of every headline number.

Behavioural modelling

Why model behaviour at all?

We can observe choices. We cannot observe the cognitive variables that drive them — the belief about reward, the value of each arm, the learning rate, the prediction error.

A behavioural model is a generative rule:

outcomes  →  latent variables  →  choices

fit to the patient's actual choices. Fitting it infers the latent variables trial-by-trial.

The point

Extract a per-trial perceived probability \( \hat p_t \) and prediction error \( \delta_t \), then regress single-neuron firing against them.

That is exactly the model → brain bridge needed for Aim 2.

Model 1

Rescorla–Wagner / Q-learning

Learn the value \(V\) of the gamble by nudging it toward each outcome:

$$\delta_t = r_t - V_t \qquad V_{t+1} = V_t + \alpha\,\delta_t$$

\(\delta_t\) = prediction error (surprise); \(\alpha\) = fixed learning rate; updated after every outcome.

Choices come from a sigmoid on the value difference:

$$P(\text{gamble})_t = \sigma\!\big(\beta\,\Delta V_t \;+\; \underbrace{\kappa\,c_{t-1}}_{\text{stickiness}} \;+\; b\big)$$

\(c_{t-1}=\pm1\) = previous choice; \(\kappa\) weights perseveration; \(b\) = overall bias.

Why it's a good baseline

Simple, few parameters, captures incremental trial-and-error learning.

Why the stickiness term

The patient is strongly perseverative and asymmetric:

0.93
win-stay
0.58
lose-shift

Clings to a winning gamble far more than it abandons a losing one — a pure value learner can't capture this.

Stickiness = choice kernel = a tendency to repeat the last action regardless of value. It's one extra parameter that pays for itself many times over (next slide).

Model 2

The Hierarchical Gaussian Filter

A Bayesian observer that infers not just a hidden, drifting reward probability, but also how fast it is drifting — its volatility — across stacked levels.

3-level binary HGF:

  • \(x_1\) — the binary outcome (reward / no reward)
  • \(x_2\) — the tendency toward reward (probability, in logit space)
  • \(x_3\) — the log-volatility of \(x_2\)

Why use it instead of RW?

RW's learning rate \(\alpha\) is fixed. The HGF's learning rate is dynamic — it is precision-weighted and rises when the environment seems uncertain or volatile.

A principled, normative account of adaptive learning: learn fast when the world is changing, slow when it's stable.

Pitch the HGF as "RW with a learning rate that knows when to speed up". It's elegant — but for this blocky task it loses to RW+stickiness, which is itself an interesting result.
Model 2 — under the hood

HGF — the key equations

Belief about reward before the outcome:

$$\hat p_t = \sigma\!\big(\mu_2^{(t-1)}\big)$$

Prediction error at the outcome \(u_t\in\{0,1\}\):

$$\delta_1^{(t)} = u_t - \hat p_t$$

Belief update with a dynamic learning rate:

$$\mu_2^{(t)} = \mu_2^{(t-1)} + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{\pi_2^{(t)}}}_{\text{learning rate}}\,\delta_1^{(t)}$$

Precision (inverse variance) couples to volatility \(x_3\) via \(\kappa,\omega_2\):

$$\hat\pi_2^{(t)} = \frac{1}{\sigma_2^{(t-1)} + \exp\!\big(\kappa\,\mu_3^{(t-1)} + \omega_2\big)}$$

Higher believed volatility → lower precision → larger learning rate.

Shared response model (same for RW & HGF — fair comparison):

$$P(\text{gamble}) = \sigma\!\big(\beta\,(\hat p\cdot 4 - 1) + b\big)$$

Free parameters: \(\omega_2\) (tonic volatility), \(\beta\) (inverse temperature), \(b\) (bias). Recovery is excellent (r ≈ 0.92–0.97); but \(\omega_2\) drift is underpowered with 8 sessions.

Reference: Mathys et al., Front. Hum. Neurosci. 2011 & 2014. \( \sigma \) = logistic sigmoid.

Where we are

  • Behaviour: a risk-averse value tracker with a strong habit.
  • Model: RW + stickiness beats the HGF here — hierarchical volatility isn't needed for blocky contingencies.
  • Neural: ~half of units carry choice / outcome information; a frontal = reward vs AG = choice double dissociation.
